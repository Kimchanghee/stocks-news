Open the Form 4 filings for almost any large company and you will see executives selling shares on a regular schedule. Many of those sales carry a footnote or a checked box saying they were made under a **Rule 10b5-1 trading plan**. Understanding these plans, and the 2023 rule changes that tightened them, is the key to reading insider-sale headlines calmly.

## What problem does Rule 10b5-1 solve?

Corporate insiders routinely know things the market does not. Trading while aware of material non-public information can be illegal insider trading. Rule 10b5-1 under the Securities Exchange Act provides an **affirmative defense**: a person can show a trade was not made "on the basis of" inside information if it was executed under a binding contract, instruction or written plan adopted **before** the person became aware of such information, and that plan specified the amount, price and date of trades, or a formula for setting them, without later influence by the person.

The practical effect is that a director can schedule sales months ahead, for example to pay taxes on vesting stock or to diversify, and have them executed even during a quiet period when the company is sitting on undisclosed news.

## What changed in 2023?

Concerns that plans were being adopted, cancelled or overlapped opportunistically led the SEC to adopt amendments in December 2022 (Release No. 33-11138), effective **February 27, 2023**. The main conditions now include:

- **Cooling-off periods.** Directors and officers must wait the later of **90 days** after adoption or modification, or **two business days** after the company discloses financial results in its 10-Q or 10-K for the fiscal quarter in which the plan was adopted, subject to a maximum of **120 days**. Other persons, except the issuer itself, must wait **30 days**.
- **Director and officer certifications.** When adopting a plan, directors and officers must certify that they are not aware of material non-public information and are adopting the plan in good faith, not as part of a scheme to evade the rule.
- **Limits on overlapping plans.** The defense is generally not available for a person who has more than one plan in effect for open-market trades during the same period, with limited exceptions such as sell-to-cover arrangements for tax withholding on vesting awards.
- **Single-trade plans.** A plan designed to execute a single trade can rely on the defense only once in any 12-month period.
- **Good-faith operation.** The person must have acted in good faith with respect to the plan throughout its life, not only at adoption.

## How do you spot a 10b5-1 trade on Form 4?

Form 4 reports changes in a director's, officer's or 10% owner's holdings and is generally due within **two business days** of the transaction. The 2022 amendments added a **checkbox** to Forms 4 and 5 to indicate whether a reported transaction was made pursuant to a contract, instruction or written plan intended to satisfy the affirmative defense of Rule 10b5-1(c). Filers also give the plan adoption date, often in a footnote.

When reading a Form 4:

1. **Check the transaction code.** "S" is an open-market or private sale, "P" a purchase, "M" an option exercise, "F" shares withheld to pay tax, "G" a gift.
2. **Check the 10b5-1 box and footnotes.** If checked, the trade was pre-scheduled; the footnote gives the plan date.
3. **Look at the holdings column.** Column 5 shows shares owned after the transaction. A sale of 2% of holdings reads differently from a sale of 80%.

## Where do companies disclose plan adoptions?

The same amendments added **Item 408 of Regulation S-K**. Each quarter, a company must disclose whether any director or officer adopted or terminated a Rule 10b5-1 plan (or a non-Rule 10b5-1 trading arrangement), with the person's name and title, the date, the plan duration and the aggregate number of securities covered. Look in **Part II, Item 5** of the 10-Q and **Item 9B** of the 10-K. Companies also disclose their insider trading policies under Item 408(b).

## Does an insider sale tell you anything?

Some general cautions, without drawing conclusions about any particular trade:

- **Scheduled sales reflect a past decision.** The relevant moment is the adoption date, at least 90 days earlier for officers.
- **Many sales are mechanical.** Tax withholding on restricted stock ("F" code) and sell-to-cover arrangements happen whether or not the insider has a view.
- **Open-market purchases are rarer.** Purchases with code "P" outside a plan involve the insider's own money at a discretionary time.
- **Context lives elsewhere.** The proxy statement (DEF 14A) shows each officer's total holdings and ownership guidelines; Schedule 13D/G shows large outside holders.

Nothing in a Form 4 is a recommendation, and our docket does not rank insider trades as signals.

## Gifts and Form 144

Two related changes are worth knowing. The 2022 amendments require gifts of securities by insiders to be reported on Form 4 within two business days, rather than deferred to an annual Form 5. Separately, affiliates selling restricted or control securities under Rule 144 file **Form 144**, which has been filed electronically on EDGAR since April 2023. A Form 144 is a notice of a proposed sale, not proof the sale happened; the Form 4 confirms execution.
